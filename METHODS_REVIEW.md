# Methods review: Pigou (1920), discriminating monopoly and railway rates

**Paper.** A. C. Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare*, London: Macmillan, 1920. Part II, chapter XIV, "Discriminating
Monopoly" (pp. 240–255); chapter XV, "The Special Problem of Railway Rates" (pp. 256–282); Appendix III, § 24
(pp. 951–952). Chapters XVII and XVIII in the fourth edition (1932).

**Statistical focus.** The classification of discrimination into three degrees with the claim that in practice "the
third degree only is found," and the railway prediction that under the "value of service principle" goods are
classed and charged mainly by their value, with the straight-line-demand results that support both.

**Review standard.** Internal consistency of the formal results, explicitness of assumptions, and the testability of
the predictions. Empirical reporting standards are applied only where the chapters make empirical statements.

**What was read.** The full text of the 1920 first edition (Internet Archive, identifier `economicsofwelfa00pigouoft`,
OCR text), chapters XIV and XV and the relevant sections of Appendix III; the table of contents and chapter
headings of the 1932 fourth edition (Internet Archive, `in.ernet.dli.2015.460552`) to confirm renumbering; the RePEc
listing of Pigou and Taussig, "Railway Rates and Joint Costs," *QJE* 27(4), 1913, pp. 687–694 (listing only, not the
text). Taussig's 1891 article was not read; it is described here only as Pigou quotes it.

## Methods summary

**Research design.** Deductive theory. Pigou defines three forms of discriminating monopoly, compares each with
simple monopoly and simple competition by its effect on output and on the "national dividend," and applies the
third form to railway rate classification. Proofs are relegated to Appendix III and stated "in a somewhat dogmatic
form" in the text (p. 240, footnote).

**Sampling strategy.** Not applicable. No sample is drawn. Illustrations (British railway classification under the
Railway Rates and Charges Act, decisions of United States railway commissioners, French wine rates, workmen's
tickets, Cambridge boatmen) are chosen to illustrate, not to test.

**Sample size.** Not applicable.

**Inclusion and exclusion criteria.** Not applicable to data. The theory excludes interdependent demands across
sub-markets by assumption (§ 8, p. 247), stating that relaxing it leaves "the broad results" substantially
unaltered without proof in the text.

**Independent and dependent variables.** In the formal model, the choice variables are outputs or prices in each
sub-market; the outcome is aggregate output relative to the "ideal output" and the values of marginal net product.
In the railway application, the implied explanatory variable is the value of the commodity and the outcome is the
rate per ton-mile.

**Statistical methods.** None. The "statistics" are the formal results: the polygon analogy for the second degree
(§ 10), the output comparisons of §§ 12–13, the straight-line footnote of chapter XV, and the straight-line proof of
Appendix III § 24.

**Reported effect sizes.** None are estimated. The numerical example of first-degree discrimination (one shilling at
the margin, 300 shillings for a bundle of 100 units) is illustrative.

**Confidence intervals.** Not applicable; no estimates.

**p-values.** Not applicable.

**Missing data treatment.** Not applicable.

**Stated limitations.** Pigou says the text states mathematical results dogmatically (p. 240); that interdependence
of demands complicates the analysis (§ 8); that the monopolist's choice of sub-markets is limited by the need for
general rules and by public opinion (§ 7); and that first-degree discrimination by separate bargaining involves
"enormous cost and trouble" (§ 6).

## Interpretation notes

- The taxonomy is definitional and internally consistent. The third degree is defined by a "practicable mark"
  separating groups, which makes it the only degree with a clear empirical counterpart in published tariffs.
- The railway prediction rests on two links that the text argues but does not demonstrate: that a shipper's demand
  price for carriage scales with the absolute value of the goods, and that railways choose classes to match.
- The claim that the third degree "only" occurs is an empirical judgment about 1920 practice under published,
  regulated tariffs. Pigou gives reasons (cost, bribery, legal exposure) rather than counts.
- The disagreement with Taussig is about interpretation. Taussig treats rate differences as joint-cost pricing that
  competition could produce; Pigou treats them as discrimination. Pigou's chapter XV footnote states that both lines
  of analysis are admissible in the joint-supply case and prefers his because it applies more generally.
- Reporting problems are of the ordinary kind for a 1920 theoretical treatise: proofs are separated from statements,
  and the empirical illustrations come without sources for any systematic comparison. No published correction or
  documented analytical error in these chapters was found in the sources read.

## Unanswered questions

- How close do actual railway classifications of the period come to a value ranking, measured across all classes
  rather than through the examples cited?
- How sensitive are the output and welfare comparisons to curved demand, beyond the straight-line case?
- How should contract pricing, which did not exist in Pigou's regulated setting, be placed in his taxonomy, given
  that it resembles first-degree separate bargaining that he judged impractical?
- How much of the rate dispersion that Pigou attributes to discrimination would remain after measuring
  movement-specific costs?

The modern rerun in this repository does not reproduce anything Pigou measured, since he measured nothing. It takes
his value-of-service prediction to 2024 rail data. Across commodity groups the markup is unrelated to commodity value;
within car types a small positive gradient appears, far below the straight-line benchmark; and most of the spread
lies inside commodities.
